Christmas Grave Flowers: Wreaths, Pots & How to Fix Them

Home › Flower Notes › Christmas Grave Flowers

By Lynn20 November 20267 min read

Here in Yorkshire, taking Christmas flowers to the grave of someone you love is a tradition many families keep every year. For some it's a quiet visit on their own; for others it's the whole family on the Sunday before Christmas, a flask of tea and a wreath. However you do it, it's a way of keeping them part of Christmas.

Every December we make grave wreaths and Christmas grave pots in the studio, and people often ask us the same practical questions: what to choose, what size, when to lay it and how to stop it blowing over. This guide answers all of them, gently and simply.

The Quick Answer

  • Choose evergreen foliage. Fir, holly, ivy and conifer cope with frost, wind and rain far better than flowers alone.
  • A 10 to 12 inch wreath suits most single headstones. For a wreath laid flat on a full-length grave, go up to around 14 inches.
  • Lay it in early to mid December. The cold keeps fresh foliage looking good for several weeks, often into January.
  • Fix it firmly. Use a wreath stand or ground pegs, and put weight in the bottom of a pot.
  • Check your cemetery's rules before you go, especially about glass, stands and when tributes are cleared.

Grave Wreath or Grave Pot?

Both are lovely, and many families choose one of each. The right one usually depends on the grave itself.

  • A grave wreath is a ring of Christmas foliage, often with a few flowers, berries, pinecones and a ribbon. It can lean against the headstone, sit on a small stand in front of it, or lie flat on the grave. The circle has always stood for everlasting love, which is why so many people choose one at Christmas.
  • A Christmas grave pot is a pot filled with winter foliage and festive touches, made to stand in front of the headstone. It's a good choice for lawn cemeteries where nothing can lie on the grass, and for graves that are very exposed to the wind, because a weighted pot stays put.
  • Flowers for the memorial vase. If the headstone has its own vase or holder, a short arrangement of foliage and flowers cut to fit is a simple, tidy option. Measure the opening before you buy or order anything.

A grave wreath and a matching Christmas grave pot. Many families lay one of each.

What Size Suits the Grave?

Headstones vary a lot, so take a quick look (or a photo on your phone) next time you visit. As a guide:

The grave What usually works
Small headstone or plaque, or a cremation plotAn 8 to 10 inch wreath, or a small pot
Standard single headstoneA 10 to 12 inch wreath against the stone or on a stand, or a medium to large pot in front
Full-length or kerbed graveA 12 to 14 inch wreath laid flat in the middle, perhaps with a pot by the headstone
Lawn cemetery (nothing on the grass)A pot or the memorial vase, standing on the headstone's base

The main thing is that it doesn't cover the inscription. If a wreath would hide the name, lay it flat or choose a pot instead.

When to Lay Christmas Grave Flowers

Most families take them in the first two weeks of December, or on the weekend before Christmas. There's no right or wrong day: some people go on a birthday or anniversary that falls in December, and some go on Christmas Eve or Christmas morning.

Fresh foliage loves the cold, so a wreath laid in early December will usually still look good on Christmas Day and often well into January. If you'd like it to look its very best for Christmas itself, the week before is ideal.

How to Fix It So It Stays Put

Wind is the biggest problem for grave flowers on our Pennine hillsides. A few simple things make all the difference:

  1. Use a wreath stand or easel. A small metal stand pushed firmly into the ground in front of the headstone holds a wreath upright without leaning anything on the stone.
  2. Peg a flat wreath down. Push two or three metal ground pegs (like tent pegs) into the soil over the wire frame on the back of the wreath. Keep them hidden under the foliage.
  3. Weigh down pots. A layer of gravel or a couple of stones in the bottom of a pot stops it tipping over. A heavy pot is better than a light plastic one.
  4. Tie it to the vase if you can. A green garden tie or cable tie around the base of an arrangement and the memorial vase keeps it secure.
  5. Don't stick anything to the headstone. Tape and adhesive hooks can mark the stone, and many cemeteries don't allow them.

Two or three ground pegs over the frame keep a flat wreath in place, even on a windy hillside.

What Lasts Best Outdoors

  • Foliage: fir, blue pine, cedar, holly, ivy and conifer all stand up to frost and wind. They're the backbone of any grave arrangement.
  • Flowers: chrysanthemums, carnations and roses last best outside in the cold. White and cream look beautiful against the dark greenery, and many families choose the person's favourite colour.
  • Finishing touches: pinecones, dried seed heads and artificial berries last all winter. Birds will often take real berries.
  • Ribbon: choose a weatherproof ribbon rather than fabric or velvet, which soaks up the rain and goes limp.

If you can't visit often, an artificial wreath is a practical choice. It won't need any looking after and will still look neat in January. Many families mix the two: a fresh wreath for Christmas and an artificial pot that stays through the winter.

Evergreen foliage with cream flowers and pinecones holds up well through frost and rain.

A Few Gentle Things to Bear in Mind

  • Check your cemetery's rules. Each cemetery has its own rules about what can be left, whether stands are allowed and when old flowers are cleared. Most avoid glass, which can break in the frost or when the grass is cut.
  • Talk to the rest of the family. If several of you visit the same grave, a quick message saves two wreaths arriving on the same day (although that's never a bad thing).
  • Keep within the plot. Neighbouring graves belong to other families, so keep stands, pots and pegs within your own space.
  • Protect your card. A message card will be ruined by the first shower. Pop it in a small clear plastic sleeve or a sandwich bag before you tuck it in.
  • Clear it when it's tired. Many families take Christmas tributes away after Twelfth Night in early January, or when they start to look past their best. Some cemeteries clear them in January, so check with the office if you'd like to keep the stand or pot.

A clear sleeve keeps your message dry, whatever the Yorkshire weather does.

Common Questions

When should I put Christmas flowers on a grave?

Most families lay them in the first two weeks of December, or on the weekend before Christmas. A fresh foliage wreath laid in early December will usually still look good on Christmas Day, because the cold keeps it fresh.

What size grave wreath do I need?

As a guide, a 10 to 12 inch wreath suits most single headstones when it's propped against the stone or on a small stand. For a wreath laid flat on a full-length or kerbed grave, a larger 14 inch wreath looks more in proportion.

How long does a fresh grave wreath last?

Outdoors in cold weather, a wreath of fresh Christmas foliage will usually look good for several weeks, often into January. Mild, wet or windy weather shortens that, and any fresh flowers in it will fade before the foliage does.

How do I stop a grave wreath blowing away?

Use a wreath stand or easel pushed firmly into the ground, or lay the wreath flat and hold it down with two or three metal ground pegs over the frame. A heavy pot with gravel or stones in the bottom stays put far better than a light plastic one.

When should Christmas grave flowers be taken away?

Many families clear them after Twelfth Night in early January, or as soon as they start to look tired. Some cemeteries clear old tributes themselves in January, so it's worth checking with the cemetery office.
