Autumn Wedding Flowers: Colours & Blooms That Work With the Season

Home › Flower Notes › Autumn Wedding Flowers: Colours & Blooms That Work With the Season

By Lynn31 August 20264 min read

Autumn is one of my favourite seasons for weddings — there's a warmth and richness to the flowers you just don't get any other time of year. Our guide to wedding flowers by season covers autumn in one paragraph among four; this post is the longer version, just for couples marrying this September or October, or already thinking ahead to next year. It's about the colours, the blooms, and how they actually look on the day, rather than a full run-through of every season.

The Autumn Palette

Where spring and summer weddings tend to lean blush, pastel and fresh green, autumn is the season to embrace something warmer — burnt orange, rust, deep burgundy, mustard and chocolate-brown all sit beautifully together, and they photograph particularly well in the lower, golden light you get in September and October. It's a palette that feels rich rather than muted, and it works with the season instead of trying to recreate a summer wedding a few months too late.

The Blooms Doing the Work

Dahlias are the backbone of most fresh, in-season autumn work — café-au-lait, deep red and rust varieties especially. Their one real limit is the weather: dahlias are one of the first flowers to go with a hard frost, so they're most reliable for weddings up to early-to-mid October outdoors. If your date is later than that, we lean more heavily on the next three instead.

Chrysanthemums are much hardier and carry the season through into November, and they come in a huge range of forms — from tight pompoms to spidery, spiky varieties — so they can do delicate detail work or big, structural blooms depending on what a bouquet needs. Amaranthus adds movement and drama, trailing in deep burgundy or rust down the front of a bouquet or off the edge of a table. And rich foliage — copper beech, rosehips, physalis (Chinese lanterns) and dried seed heads or wheat — is what pulls the whole thing together and gives it that unmistakably autumnal, textural feel.

Café-au-lait and rust dahlias with trailing amaranthus and copper beech — a typical early-autumn bridal bouquet.

Three Arrangements We Might Put Together This Autumn

To make this less abstract, here are three examples of the kind of thing we'd realistically build for an autumn wedding, depending on the brief:

  • A bridal bouquet — rust and café-au-lait dahlias as the focal blooms, deep burgundy chrysanthemums for structure, trailing amaranthus for movement, and copper beech and eucalyptus foliage to soften the whole shape.
  • A long table centrepiece for a barn reception — low, loose arrangements running down a wooden table in rust, mustard and burgundy, mixing chrysanthemums and dahlias with dried wheat, physalis and seed heads, styled in wooden crates or dark glass rather than formal vases.
  • A pedestal display for a grand entrance hall — the same warm palette but built taller and more structured, with deep burgundy and chocolate-brown chrysanthemums doing more of the work than dahlias, since they hold their shape better in a formal, statement display.

A low, textural centrepiece built for a long reception table, mixing fresh blooms with dried seed heads and wheat.

How the Palette Photographs

This is where an autumn palette really earns its keep. Rust, burgundy and burnt orange sit naturally alongside the exposed wood, stone and dark beams of a typical barn venue, so the flowers read as part of the room rather than an addition to it. In a grander setting — a country house or stately home with more formal, neutral interiors — the same palette does the opposite job, giving the room a real jewel-toned lift against the muted stone and panelling. Either way, the warm, golden light of an autumn afternoon flatters these tones in a way that's much harder to get right with the paler palettes of spring and summer.

A Few Practical Notes

  • If dahlias matter to you, aim for a date before mid-October if the wedding is outdoors, or check with us early if it's later — the first frost decides that timeline, not the calendar.
  • Bring a fabric swatch or a photo of your venue when we meet, the same as any season — "rust" and "burnt orange" mean something slightly different to everyone.
  • Book earlier than you might think. Autumn is one of our busiest periods, and it runs straight into our Christmas commissions from November onwards, so the sooner we lock in your date, the more choice you'll have.
